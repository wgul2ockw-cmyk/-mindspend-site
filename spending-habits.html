Improve spending habits by understanding the moment.

Improving financial behavior starts with an observable decision. Instead of deciding that you are “bad with money,” record what happened before a purchase and choose one small change you can review next week.

Separate what you bought from how you decided

An expense category answers “what was it?” A planned or impulse tag answers “when did I decide?” Neither is a moral score. An unplanned purchase can meet a real need, and a planned purchase can still cost more than you can afford.

MindSpend uses this distinction as a prompt for reflection. The label records how you understood the decision at that moment; your interpretation may change when you revisit the context. Read the original Thai explanation of planned versus impulse spending.

Try a four-step weekly spending review

  1. Capture a specific event. Write the amount, item, and whether you planned to buy it before encountering the offer.
  2. Add context in one sentence. Note the situation: a delayed meal, a sale notification, a friend’s invitation, or a recurring bill. You do not have to record sensitive details.
  3. Choose a small experiment. For one type of non-essential purchase, make a list before shopping or revisit the item the next day. Decide in advance when the pause would be inappropriate, such as an urgent necessary expense.
  4. Review the result. Did you follow the plan? Did it make the decision easier? Did the cost move elsewhere? Keep, change, or stop the experiment based on what happened.

Treat the review as a small personal experiment. Compare several opportunities to make the same decision: a quieter week, changed income, or fewer chances to buy can affect the result too.

A concrete impulse-spending example

Illustration · a purchase triggered by a sale alert
ObservationA testable next step
“I bought a 900-baht item after a flash-sale notification.”Record the event without labelling yourself.
“I had not planned to buy it.”For similar non-essential items, add it to a list and revisit tomorrow.
“I still want it the next day.”Check whether it fits the money available before deciding.
“I bought less online but spent more elsewhere.”Review the whole week, not only the category you were watching.

A pause gives you another decision point; it is not a rule to delay food, medication, or other urgent needs. Buying the item after reviewing it can still be a deliberate choice.

Track a habit without turning it into a score

Choose a measure that describes an action: days with complete entries, weekly reviews completed, or purchases checked against a list. Keep amounts alongside the context. A lower impulse percentage alone does not prove better financial health; missing entries or relabelling decisions could create the same result.

For a wider concept, the CFPB financial well-being resource considers how people experience their financial situation. MindSpend’s planned/impulse tags are not that questionnaire and should not be presented as its score.

Start with the free expense log, then connect the review to a specific goal in the financial freedom guide. If your income cannot cover essential costs, the issue also requires a cash-flow response; reflecting on small purchases alone will not close the gap.

Money habit questions

How do I start mindful spending?

Pick one recent purchase, write what happened, and identify one decision you could approach differently next time. Use a routine short enough to repeat.

Does every impulse purchase need to stop?

No. The tag helps you notice a decision. Consider the need, affordability, and consequences instead of treating all spontaneity as a failure.

How long does changing a money habit take?

There is no promised timeline here. Review a small action over repeated opportunities, and change the plan when the situation or your needs change.
